Author mode

For anyone who has a book in them but thought writing one meant sitting down to write. Your thoughts, pieced together, already are one.

00 · Overview

Author mode is a small addition to Wispr Flow that turns spoken thoughts into a book. Ideas start in Scratchpad. One of them becomes a book with Notes, Draft and Preview. Chapters appear only when the author names them, edits happen by voice against numbered paragraphs, and the whole thing reads back as pages. Nobody has to type, or call themselves a writer.

01 · The spark

A whole book went through Flow. Most of the work still happened outside it.

Wispr’s CEO, Tanay Kothari, shared that Robert Greene wrote his new book without typing a word.

After a stroke in 2018 left him unable to use his right side, Greene wrote by hand across about a hundred notebooks, then used Flow to turn them into a manuscript. He described the process as excruciating and exciting at once. When I mapped out his loop, only one step really lived inside Flow:

02 · The belief

Writing a book shouldn’t require writing.

We picture writing a book as a physical act: a desk, a keyboard, a blank page, months of typing. So most people who have a book in them never start.

But a book is really just thoughts, put in order. Everyone has thoughts, and most of us say them out loud every day, to friends, in voice notes, on long walks. The hard part was never having them. It was the typing.

Flow already captures how people think out loud. Today those thoughts land in a feed, grouped by date, and scroll away. I wanted Flow to help piece them together, until one day someone looks back and realises they’ve written a book.

03 · Who it’s for

You don’t have to call yourself an author.

This was never meant to be only for authors. It’s for anyone whose thoughts could become a book, if writing one didn’t mean writing.

  • The storytellerA grandparent with sixty years of stories nobody has written down.
  • The explainerA founder or teacher who makes more sense out loud than on paper.
  • The one who can’t typeStroke, RSI, low vision, dyslexia, or simply not for hours at a time.
  • The beginnerSomeone with a few vague ideas and no idea where a book would even begin.
04 · Research

Nobody starts with Chapter 1.

My first sketch had chapters on day one. Before building it, I looked at how books actually come together, and that idea didn’t survive. Two camps come up again and again, and most people sit somewhere between them:

Plotters

Outline first: premise, characters, major turns. They want a place for notes before drafting.

Discovery writers

Write to find out what the story is. Structure shows up in the draft, not before it.

Scrivener’s lesson

The tool long-form writers love lets you write loose scenes and gather them into chapters later.

So Author mode follows the order books are really made in:

Spark→Gather→Draft→Shape→Read
05 · Principles

Four rules I designed against.

Meet ideas where they already are

Thoughts already land in Scratchpad. Build on it, don’t add a new place to learn.

Structure is discovered

Never ask for chapters up front. Let them be named once the shape appears.

Everything works by voice

If someone can’t use their hands, they can still write, navigate and edit the whole book.

Never lose your place

Always show where your voice is going, and keep paper and screen numbered the same.

06 · The solution

From a vague idea to pages you can read.

Author mode sits in Flow’s sidebar, under Scratchpad. Here is the whole path, using a story that began as one rambling note about a lighthouse keeper.

6.1Spark: ideas start in Scratchpad

Rough thoughts are dictated or typed into Flow’s existing Scratchpad, in the same cards with the same hover toolbar. One new icon joins edit, pin and delete. It opens a small menu: add this note to a book you’ve started, or start a new book from it. Or just say build it into a book.

6.2Gather: starting a book doesn’t pull you away

Ideas come in bursts, so you stay in Scratchpad. A toast confirms the new book with an Open button, and the note gets a small tag showing where it went. Inside the book, Notes is the first tab: premise, characters, scraps. Plotters outline here; discovery writers skip straight to Draft.

6.3Draft: talk it into existence

The draft is a list of loose sections. Untitled ones show their first words, so they’re findable without names. The footer always says where your voice is going (“Dictating into §1”), and the mic is a toggle, not a hold, so it works one-handed or hands-free. new section new paragraph go to section 2

6.4Shape: name it when it shows up

Give a section a title and it becomes a chapter, numbered automatically. Untitled sections stay as scene breaks inside the chapter before them. Structure is something you discover and then confirm. new chapter The Tin

6.5Revise: edit by paragraph number, by voice

This closes step 4 of Greene’s loop. Every paragraph is numbered on screen and on the printout. In Revise mode you speak an edit against a number. The paragraph flashes and shows the old text struck through, and undo brings it back. Edit commands only run in Revise, so prose that happens to start with “replace…” is never mistaken for one.

replace paragraph 2 with …rewrite it
in paragraph 2 change X to Yswap a phrase
insert after paragraph 1 …add a beat
delete paragraph 3cut it

6.6Read: then read it like a book

Preview lays the manuscript out as 6 × 9 pages in one continuous scroll: a title page, each chapter on a new page, a ⁂ between scenes. It’s what Greene printed for. The same layout prints, with ¶ numbers in the margin that match Revise, and exports to Markdown.

07 · Decisions

Small on purpose.

Its own place in the sidebar

A book is somewhere you return to for months. My first version was a toggle on the Dictation page, and it buried the feature. It now has a home next to Scratchpad.

Building a book doesn’t redirect you

The first version jumped straight into the new book, which broke the flow of jotting ideas. Now it stays put and offers “Open”.

Sections before chapters

Matches how plotters and discovery writers both work. Naming a section is the only step needed to make a chapter.

A toggle mic, and voice for navigation too

Holding a key assumes a free hand. Every action, including moving around the book, has a spoken form.

Paragraph numbers that match paper

Print and screen share one numbering, so handwritten margin notes become spoken edits directly.

08 · Where it stands

A working prototype, built in Flow’s visual language.

It runs in the browser with Flow’s fonts and colours and Chrome’s speech engine. It proves the flow, not Flow’s transcription quality.

Works today

  • Scratchpad → book, by menu or voice
  • Notes, Draft and Preview per book
  • 12 voice commands for navigation and editing
  • Undo, autosave, print and .md export

Left out on purpose

  • Real Flow integration and system-wide hotkey
  • AI rewriting (Transforms already cover it)
  • Sync, accounts and collaboration
  • .docx export and version history

Next, if this lands

  • Flow’s own transcription for punctuation and cleanup
  • Reorder sections by voice
  • Import photographed notebook pages
  • Test with people who’ve never written anything
09 · Reflection

The feature isn’t the dictation.

Flow already turns speech into text beautifully. What was missing was somewhere for those thoughts to go, and permission for them to stay messy until they find their shape.

The biggest change along the way came from questioning my own first instinct. I started with Chapter 1, Chapter 2. Looking at how books really begin showed that structure has to be discovered, and that changed everything after it: the binder, the naming, even what counts as a chapter.

If Author mode works, its users won’t think of themselves as authors when they start. They’ll just have talked, for long enough, about something they cared about.
